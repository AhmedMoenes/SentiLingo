2. Literature Review

Related research and existing solutions, what SentiLingo borrows from them, and where it differs.

2.1 Review Method

We searched the ACL Anthology, Google Scholar, arXiv and vendor documentation for work on the sentiment of reviews and social-media text, focusing on Arabic, Egyptian dialect and multilingual models. Every figure quoted below was checked against the original source (October 2026).

2.2 Background and Challenges

Sentiment analysis classifies the opinion expressed in a text. Customer feedback makes this hard: messages are short and informal, often mix praise and complaints, and neutral messages are hard to label consistently. Arabic adds further difficulties:

  • rich morphology and inconsistent spelling (e.g. أ/ا, ى/ي);
  • dialects such as Egyptian Arabic that differ from MSA in vocabulary and grammar;
  • Franco-Arabic and code-switching with English, for which very few labelled resources exist (Hamed et al., 2025);
  • sarcasm and label noise, the main sources of the errors that remain in Arabic sentiment models (Abu Farha & Magdy, 2021).

2.3 Classical Approaches

Lexicon-based tools such as VADER score text with a hand-built word list plus rules for negation and intensity. They need no training data but are English-only (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014).

Supervised learning does better:

  • On movie reviews, Naive Bayes and SVM classifiers on word features reached 81–83% accuracy and beat keyword lists (Pang et al., 2002).
  • Adding word bigrams lifted a Naive Bayes–SVM hybrid to 91.2% (Wang & Manning, 2012).
  • For Arabic, an SVM on TF-IDF n-grams scored 0.63 F1 on the four-class Egyptian tweet corpus ASTD (Nabil et al., 2015).
  • The best Arabic system at SemEval-2017 was a Naive Bayes model (Rosenthal et al., 2017).

These models are fast, cheap, interpretable and effective with modest data. However, they see no context beyond n-grams, are weak on negation and sarcasm, and break on unseen dialect spellings. This is the baseline required by the brief; SentiLingo adds character n-grams to handle spelling variation.

2.4 Deep Learning and Transformers

LSTMs (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) read text as a sequence and can learn effects such as the scope of a negation. They need more data and tuning than linear models.

Pretrained Transformers are first trained on large text corpora and then fine-tuned for the task:

  • BERT reached 94.9% on a standard English sentiment benchmark (Devlin et al., 2019).
  • XLM-R extends this approach to 100 languages (Conneau et al., 2020).
  • XLM-T, a Twitter-adapted version of XLM-R, reached macro-F1 66.9 on Arabic and 70.6 on English three-class tweets (Barbieri et al., 2022).

Arabic-specific models do better on Arabic:

  • AraBERT reached 92.6% accuracy on the balanced ASTD, against 80.1% for multilingual BERT (Antoun et al., 2020).
  • MARBERT, pretrained on one billion dialect-rich tweets, beat both XLM-R and multilingual BERT on SemEval-2017 Arabic (Abdul-Mageed et al., 2021).

Large language models can classify sentiment without any training. They do well on simple tasks but trail fine-tuned smaller models on harder ones, and they cost much more per prediction (Zhang et al., 2024).

2.5 Arabic and Egyptian-Dialect Studies

The study closest to SentiLingo compared a TF-IDF SVM, an LSTM, multilingual BERT and Arabic BERT models on 40,000 Egyptian tweets (Abu Kwaik et al., 2022). The SVM (0.84 accuracy) matched multilingual BERT (0.83), a Twitter-adapted Arabic BERT was best (0.91) and the LSTM was weakest (0.49); the study used two classes only. A re-implementation of published Arabic methods found that Transformers gave the best F-scores: 0.69 on SemEval-2017 and 0.76 on ASTD (Abu Farha & Magdy, 2021). For code-switched feedback, a corpus of 4,100 Egyptian Arabic–English comments reached an F1 of about 0.93, both with neural models and with a fine-tuned GPT-3.5 (Sherif & Sabty, 2025).

2.8 Lessons Applied

  1. Tune the classical baseline carefully, with word and character n-grams, and keep negation words.
  2. Compare a multilingual Transformer with a dialect-aware Arabic model.
  3. Report macro-F1 and per-class metrics, with realistic targets: about 0.67–0.71 macro-F1 is already strong for three-class tweets.
